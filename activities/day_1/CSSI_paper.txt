Three-dimensional coherent X-ray surface
scattering imaging near total external reﬂection
Tao Sun1 *, Zhang Jiang1, Joseph Strzalka1, Leonidas Ocola2 and Jin Wang1 *
Lensless X-ray coherent diffraction imaging (CDI) has emerged
as a thriving ﬁeld promising applications in materials and biological sciences1–13 with a theoretical imaging resolution only
limited by the X-ray wavelength. Most CDI methods use transmission geometry, which is not suitable for nanostructures
grown on opaque substrates or for objects of interest comprising only surfaces or interfaces. Attempts have been made to
perform CDI experiments in reﬂection geometry, both optically
and with X-rays, but the reconstruction resulted in mostly
planar images, with less success in the third dimension14,15.
Here, we discuss the development of coherent surface scattering imaging in grazing-incidence geometry that takes advantage of enhanced X-ray surface scattering and interference
near total external reﬂection. We demonstrate the successful
reconstruction of substrate-supported non-periodic surface
patterns in three dimensions with nanometre resolution in
the direction normal to the substrate, promising wide applications in elucidating structures in substrate-supported and
buried nanoelectronics and photonics.
For samples such as thin ﬁlms, nanopatterns and buried interfaces,
reﬂection-mode coherent diffraction imaging (CDI) can be more
successful than transmission-mode CDI. Previous studies using
reﬂection-mode CDI implied that the results were not sufﬁcient to
generate true three-dimensional reconstruction of nanostructures at
surfaces. To obtain unambiguous structural information in the
third dimension normal to the substrate, we collected coherent
surface scattering patterns from substrate-supported nanostructures
at grazing-incidence angles in the vicinity of the sample critical
angle (typically a fraction of a degree for hard X-rays), as shown in
Fig. 1a. In this angular region, in-plane scattering is greatly enhanced
as a result of total external reﬂection. As demonstrated in many X-ray
studies, including reﬂectivity and diffuse scattering at grazing incidence, surface scattering is particularly sensitive to the sample electron density distribution in the plane and in the direction
perpendicular to the substrate surface16–20, which allows depthresolved structural characterization to be achieved by varying the incident angle. At grazing incidence, the momentum transfer along the
surface normal (z or qz direction) spans several orders of magnitude
from 0.001 to .1 nm21, which enables high-resolution reconstruction of sample structures along the surface normal direction. At
angles far beyond the critical angle, the scattering intensity (either
coherent or incoherent) decreases algebraically with incident angle
(or qz). The grazing-incidence coherent surface scattering imaging
(CSSI) technique is therefore particularly suitable for weakly scattering non-periodic objects, as it ensures a large signal-to-noise ratio
within a reasonable data collection time. Another advantage of
grazing-incidence CSSI over high-angle reﬂection-mode CDI is its
larger detectable area (a consequence of geometry), albeit with a correspondingly reduced resolution. All these unique scattering characteristics in the near total external reﬂection region promise an

unprecedented surface sensitivity for mapping scattering intensity
in reciprocal space and reconstructing the corresponding surface
structures in three dimensions.
To demonstrate the grazing-incidence CSSI concept, we ﬁrst
examined a simple gold double-stripe structure, patterned on a
3-mm-thick ﬂat silicon substrate along the x-direction. Figure 1b
shows the scattering pattern obtained at an incident angle of
ai ¼ 0.68, which is above the silicon critical angle (0.2458 at E ¼
7.35 keV) and just below the gold critical angle (0.618). In contrast
to conventional transmission CDI, where most incident X-rays
penetrate through the sample without participating in diffraction
events, the specular reﬂection (at qx and qy ≈ 0) in CSSI is part
of the sample scattering pattern, similar to the results in
Bragg-diffraction-based CDI4. Accordingly, no beam stop is
required to block the spot, which allows low-q information to
be limited only by the instrument resolution (dqx ≈ 7.6 ×
1026 nm21, dqy ≈ 7.6 × 1024 nm21). The double-stripe conﬁguration is analogous to that of the classic Young’s double-slit experiment, where waves from two slits interfere to generate intensity
modulations on the detection screen; however, there are several
major different and also distinct characteristics. First, in addition
to the X-rays being strongly reﬂected by the two gold stripes,
weak scattering by the silicon substrate contributes to the interference process. Second, with the grazing-incidence geometry, the
horizontal axis of the scattering pattern represents the in-plane
y-component of momentum transfer in the small-angle region,
and the vertical axis corresponds to both qz and qx components.
Also, unlike in the transmission geometry, the qx component is
not linearly represented by the vertical pixel position because of
the way the Ewald sphere intercepts the reciprocal lattice (for a
detailed explanation see Supplementary Section S1). This causes
the scattering pattern shown in Fig. 1b to bend downwards at the
large azimuthal scattering angle 2uf.
Figure 1c (open circles) shows the scattering intensity proﬁle
along qy , a line scan at qx ¼ 0 from the scattering pattern displayed
in Fig. 1b. The simulation was based on Young’s double-slit experiment with a partially coherent X-ray beam. Numerically, the partial
coherence was taken into account by the convolution of the instrument resolution and the ideal scattering curve21. Details of the
simulation are presented in Supplementary Section S2. The
remarkably good agreement between the data and the simulation
demonstrates the feasibility of coherent surface scattering in the
grazing-incidence geometry. One can see that the projected transverse coherence length extends over the millimetre-long stripes
along the beam direction. Similar to conventional CDI methods,
the CSSI scattering pattern can also be used for reconstructing the
exit surface wave (ESW) by using phase-retrieval algorithms, such
as hybrid input–output (HIO)22 and error reduction (ER)23. For
reconstruction of a planar image on the x–y plane, the data must

With these complex patterns, scattering speckles cover relatively
large ranges of qx and qz , both of which are represented in the vertical directions in the scattering patterns. In addition, even at constant qx , the scattering intensity drops as the incident or exit angle
increases. Accordingly, the isolation of qx and qz contributions
and the performance of intensity normalization with respect to
the incident and exit angles are prerequisites only for the twodimensional image reconstruction (see below and Supplementary
Section S3). We therefore collected scattering patterns at different
incident angles and measured the variation of the off-specular scattering intensity at a ﬁxed (qx , qy) spot, as shown in Fig. 3a,b. The
dependence of scattering intensity on incident angle demonstrates
the effectiveness of data collection under the grazing-incidence condition, where the scattering intensity is orders of magnitude higher
than that collected at higher incident angles.
To simulate the intensity curve in Fig. 3b, we developed a formfactor-independent model by combining the dynamical theory of
reﬂectivity and the interference function of reﬂected waves.

model does not account for any in-plane structural details of the
sample. Instead, we directly model the interference of the ESWs at

waves: one from gold and another from silicon. The interference
of these two waves is calculated using Parratt formalism16, with
the path difference for the two waves propagating to the same
spot on the detector. (Details of the model are provided in
Supplementary Section S3.) The simulated result is shown in
Fig. 3b as a solid line, and matches the data extremely well. With

As found in an abundance of traditional reﬂectivity experiments,
the additional qz dependence of the coherent scattering patterns
during the angular scan strongly suggests that three-dimensional
structural information is contained in the data set. To decipher
the angular dependence, we collected a series of scattering data
from the pattern at different incident angles ranging from 0.568 to
0.98. The scattering patterns from lower incident angles were
not counted for three-dimensional reconstruction because the
Born approximation alone may not be sufﬁcient to interpret the
wave ﬁelds and scattering below the critical angles. The threedimensional reciprocal-space data, shown in Fig. 4a, was constructed by ﬁlling the voxels in (qx , qy , qz) space with the
corresponding speckle intensity from each two-dimensional scattering pattern. Here, the three-dimensional reciprocal pattern is shown
by an iso-surface rendering effect—low-intensity voxels do not show
up in the plot, which leads to a seemingly smaller q range than in the
actual data collected and used for reconstruction. The reconstructed
image in two dimensions could be used as support for the x–y plane
in the three-dimensional reconstruction. For the z-direction, the
support dimension was estimated by a Fourier transform of the
specular reﬂection intensity curve (that is, Ispecular vs qz) after multiplying by q4z , analogous to obtaining a thickness value from reﬂectivity data using the Fourier method24. The reconstructed threedimensional image is shown in Fig. 4b,c (different viewing angles).
The magnitude of the ESW amplitude at each z-location
(between the two arrows in Fig. 4c) was extracted and plotted in
Fig. 4d. The error bar of each datum point represents the standard
deviation of intensities from 300 pixel locations in the x–y plane.
The intensity proﬁle shows a peak with an extended plateau,
which was ﬁt by a ﬂat-top Gaussian function, yielding a full-width
at half-maximum (FWHM) of 36.9 nm, which is consistent with
the thickness of the gold layer. The ﬂat-top Gaussian proﬁle is a
model-independent ﬁt to the ESW amplitude in the z-direction
reconstructed from the limited q range involved in the inverse
Fourier transform. This is analogous to reconstructing a square
wave with truncated reciprocal data in the Fourier space. The
slope on each side of the ﬂat top represents the resolution of the
height reconstruction, which should be approximately symmetrical.

Even though the pattern studied here has a simple structure along
the z-direction (that is, uniform thickness), there is no fundamental
limit for applying the CSSI technique to reconstruct more complex
three-dimensional architectures. However, scattering intensity
redistribution and normalization will involve iterative application
of the distorted-wave Born approximation17,25 when the layered
structure exhibits more complex scattering phenomena, such as
wave-guide effects26, near total external reﬂection.
At grazing incidence, spatial resolutions in the x-, y- and z-directions are highly anisotropic. Speciﬁcally, for a Coolsnap HQ2 detector placed 0.7 m away from the sample, the x and y resolutions at an
incident angle of 0.68 and with nine detector frame tiles are 2.6 mm
and 22 nm, respectively, and a scan of incident angle to 0.98 gives a z
resolution of 2.7 nm. As determined by the detectable q ranges, the
real-space resolutions are limited by the coherent ﬂux of the incident beam and the efﬁciency of the detector. Hypothetically, if the
coherent ﬂux is 1 × 1012 photons/s (103 times higher than the
current case), the maximum q where the scattering intensity
can be detected will be 10 times larger, leading to approximately
10 times higher real-space resolutions in all three directions. In
addition, detectors with higher quantum efﬁciency and dynamic
range and a lower noise level can improve the resolution. Even
with these improvements, the resolution in the x-direction can
still be one order of magnitude lower than that of the y-direction.
One potential remedy is to take scattering images at several azimuthal angles around the sample normal direction to provide
access to a wider in-plane q range for higher resolution. For structures composed of lighter elements, such as carbon, aluminium
and iron, the detectable q range will be smaller than that of a
strong scatterer like gold.
To conclude, we have developed a novel, optics-free, hard X-ray
imaging technique by synergistically combining coherent surface
scattering and grazing incidence near the sample total external
reﬂection. By visualizing non-periodic gold nanostructures on a
silicon substrate, we have demonstrated that CSSI is capable of
reconstructing not only planar images of surface structures, but
also three-dimensional architectures with nanometre resolution
along the surface normal. Moreover, CSSI can be readily adapted
to other coherent imaging techniques, such as resonant CDI,
Fresnel CDI, holography, ptychography and ankylography, to
study three-dimensional nanoscaled structures. Thanks to enhanced
scattering near total external reﬂection, even with a hard X-ray
coherent ﬂux of 1 × 109 photons/s, our three-dimensional reconstruction of the images is practical. It should pave the way for nanostructure visualization in biology, materials science and condensed
matter physics. With higher resolution it will ﬁnd applications in
time-resolved studies of biological membranes and supramolecules
in an aqueous environment, thin ﬁlm and quantum dot growth at

Methods
The samples studied in the present work were prepared in the cleanroom at the
Center for Nanoscale Materials, Argonne National Laboratory. Nano- and
microstructures of the gold top layer and a titanium adhesion layer were fabricated
on ﬂat silicon substrates using electron-beam lithography and sputtering techniques.
The double-stripe pattern had a nominal line width of 850 nm, separation of 2 mm
and length of 2 mm. The overall length of the logo pattern was 391 mm (x-direction),
while the maximum width of the entire pattern was 5.1 mm ( y-direction) and the
width of the thinnest line in the pattern was 350 nm. For both samples, the thickness
of the gold layer was 36 nm and the titanium adhesion layer between the gold layer
and the silicon substrate was 7 nm thick.
The grazing-incidence CSSI experiments were performed at the 8-ID-E beamline
of the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. The monochromatic
incident beam (photon energy E ¼ 7.35 keV with DE/E ¼ 2 × 1024) along the
x-direction (see Fig. 1) was collimated by three sets of slit pairs to obtain an
approximate Gaussian intensity distribution in the y–z plane with a FWHM of
15 mm in both the horizontal and vertical directions. The transverse coherence
length along the horizontal direction was 7 mm and was fully coherent across the
entire vertical direction over the 15 mm beam. The longitudinal coherence length
in our experiment was 1 mm, sufﬁcient for the grazing-incidence small-angle
scattering geometry. A detailed discussion on coherence is included in
Supplementary Section S5. The ﬂux was typically 1 × 109 photons/s for all
experiments. The incident angles ranged from 0.35 to 0.98, around the critical angle
of gold (ac ¼ 0.618 at l ¼ 0.1687 nm). A Coolsnap HQ2 detector (1,040 × 1,392
pixels, 6.45 × 6.45 mm2 per pixel and 12-bit readout) was placed 0.7 m away from

the sample for collecting scattering patterns. A ×5 lens was used, resulting in an
effective pixel dimension of 1.29 × 1.29 mm2 on the detector. The scintillator crystal
was a 5-mm-thick ﬁlm of yttrium aluminium garnet doped with cerium (YAG:Ce).
The overall detection efﬁciency of the detector system was estimated to be 0.13%
(750 photons/count). The scattering pattern from the double-stripe structure was
averaged over 40 exposures with 5 s exposure time each, and the scattering pattern
from the logo structure was averaged over ﬁve exposures with 45 s exposure
time each.
Our CSSI set-up had no optics between the slits and the detector, allowing
our imaging technique to be easily adopted by any hard or soft X-ray scattering
beamline where coherence of the source is preserved. In the grazing-incidence
geometry, the sample functions as the beam stop, because the strong incident beam
is sufﬁciently attenuated by the substrate. Therefore, extremely low-q (qx ≈ 7.6 ×
1026 nm21, qy ≈ 7.6 × 1024 nm21) scattering intensity is available in CSSI
providing the ‘support’ or maximum feature size during both two- and threedimensional reconstructions.
For phase retrieval, each reconstruction contained 100 iterations, which
alternated between nine applications of the HIO algorithm and one application of
the ER algorithm (except for the last iteration). During iteration, those pixels with
an intensity lower than a certain threshold, including the void voxels in a threedimensional reciprocal pattern, were masked out and allowed to ﬂoat. Note that
the data for three-dimensional reconstruction were multiplied by q2z before
performing reconstruction as a calibration of the algebraic decay of the diffuse
scattering intensity with qz (ref. 17). The positivity constraint was also applied for the
three-dimensional reconstruction. The ﬁnal results for both two- and threedimensional cases were averaged over 100 total reconstructions, each starting with a
random phase and random value of the feedback parameter b in the HIO algorithm
(b ≈ 0.6–0.9), and then normalized with the intensity proﬁle of the incident beam.
In our experiment, because the beam size was larger than the transverse coherence
length in the y-direction, the ‘specular’ reﬂection spot contained incoherent and
coherent scattering intensity beyond the instrument resolution. The specular
reﬂection region was therefore also masked out using a numerical beam stop with a
maximum diameter of 25 pixels. Even so, the scattering pattern provided sufﬁcient
low-q information (qx ≈ 1 × 1025 nm21, qy ¼ 8.8 × 1024 nm21).

For two-dimensional imaging, two competing factors need to be considered
when selecting the incident angle for a particular sample structure: detectable qx
range and off-specular scattering intensity. In grazing-incidence geometry, a smaller
incident angle yields a smaller range of qx , and therefore lower real-space resolution
in the x-direction. Thus, a relatively large incident angle is favourable. Plots of the
detectable q range at different incident angles are shown in Supplementary
Section S1. On the other hand, because the scattering intensity decreases with qz ,
with a relatively low incident coherent photon ﬂux as in this study, a smaller incident
angle is desirable to achieve a high signal-to-noise ratio in a reasonable amount of
data collection time. Taking the logo structure as an example, the optimized incident
angle for the logo structure lies around 0.558 in our experiment, where the q range is
large enough to give the necessary x resolution and the off-specular scattering
intensity reaches a local maximum due to a constructive interference between the
surface scattering signals from the sample.
